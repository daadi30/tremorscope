How it works

1. Sensing

The phone's MEMS accelerometer (linear acceleration, m/s², gravity removed) and gyroscope (rotation rate, °/s) are read at about 60 Hz through the browser's DeviceMotion API. By Nyquist, 60 Hz sampling captures tremors up to 30 Hz, well above the 3–12 Hz band of interest.

2. Pre-processing

  • Resample to a uniform 50 Hz (browser timestamps jitter).
  • Remove the mean, then apply a zero-phase 2nd-order Butterworth high-pass at 0.5 Hz to remove drift and gravity residue, plus a low-pass at 18 Hz.

3. Spectral analysis

Welch's method: 256-sample Hann-windowed segments, 50% overlap, zero-padded 2048-point FFT. The power spectra of the x, y and z axes are summed so the result is independent of phone orientation. The peak in 3–15 Hz is refined by parabolic interpolation.

4. Features

5. Clinical interpretation

TremorFrequencyCondition
Rest4–6 HzParkinson's disease
Postural / kinetic4–12 Hz (often 6–8)Essential tremor
Postural, low amplitude8–12 HzPhysiological (normal)
Postural, higher amplitude8–12 HzEnhanced physiological (caffeine, anxiety, thyroid)

Key discriminator: Parkinson's tremor is strongest at rest and dampens with posture, while essential tremor worsens with posture. The report compares both.

6. Spiral analysis

Touch points are converted to polar coordinates (r, θ) around the spiral centre, θ is unwrapped, and an ideal Archimedean spiral r = a + bθ is fitted by least squares. Residuals are high-pass filtered (±200 ms moving-mean subtraction) to isolate tremor wobble from slow drawing error, then normalised to the loop spacing (2πb) and spectrally analysed. The grade is a heuristic 0–4 scale inspired by the Bain–Findley clinical spiral rating.

Limitations

  • Screening and education tool only. Not a medical device and not diagnostic.
  • Amplitude thresholds are heuristic and uncalibrated across phone models.
  • Frequency bands overlap between conditions; clinical context matters.
  • Phone mass slightly damps tremor; a grip that varies between tests adds variance.
